“417 calories” was always a guess. We just stop pretending.

Nobody weighs the oil in a restaurant stir-fry. Every tracker on your phone is estimating — it just hides that behind a decimal point. Lazy Macros shows you the low and the high, so you can see when a meal is genuinely uncertain and when it isn’t.

The range narrows as the food gets more specific. “A sandwich” is a wide guess. “100 g grilled chicken breast” is a tight one. Either way, you spent five seconds logging it.

Three taps, not three minutes

No barcode hunting, no database scrolling, no “was that serving 1 cup or 1 container?”

Everything else, kept deliberately small

The features that earn their place on a screen you look at four times a day.

Snap to Log

Photograph a meal and get one entry per food, each with its own portion and macro range. Correct it in plain English and it re-estimates the whole plate.

Targets that follow your weight

BMR and TDEE recalculate from the weight you logged that day and the activity level you picked. Choose a goal from maintain up to 1 kg per week and the daily target moves with you.

Only the macros you care about

Calories and protein always. Carbs, fat, fiber, sugar, saturated fat and sodium are yours to switch on and off — the whole app follows, including what the estimator is asked for.

Plain-language day cards

“180–240 over target.” “Protein met.” No mental arithmetic against a number you have to remember, and no red screen because you were forty calories out.

Two opinions on hard foods

Run a second model as a cross-check. When the two estimates disagree by more than your threshold, the entry gets flagged instead of quietly averaging a bad guess.

Dark, light, or whatever your phone says

A real dark theme for logging dinner in bed, a real light one for daylight, and a system setting that just follows along.

Did I eat roughly the right amount, and did I get enough protein? Everything else is one tap away, and stays out of the way until you ask for it.

Your food log is yours

A diet log is personal data. Here is exactly where it goes.

Works offline first

Entries save to the device immediately and read back from a local cache, so the app works on a plane or in a basement restaurant. Cloud sync catches up when you’re back online.

Your own API key

Estimates run against your key with the provider you choose. The key stays on your device — it is never synced to the cloud with the rest of your settings.

No key? No problem

Manual mode lets you type the numbers yourself, and every estimate is editable regardless. The tracker works without an AI provider attached at all.

No ads, no upsell

Nothing to subscribe to, no premium tier hiding your own history behind a paywall, and no advertiser being told what you ate.

Open source

The whole app is a few files of plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Read it, audit the prompts, fork it, or run your own copy.

Export by owning it

Your data lives in your own storage. Nothing about the format is proprietary, and there’s no leaving-fee for taking it with you.

Questions worth asking

How accurate is this, really?

As accurate as a careful human guess, which is roughly what every tracker gives you once you stop weighing food. The difference is that Lazy Macros shows you the uncertainty rather than rounding it away. For a well-defined food the range is tight; for “a bowl of pasta” it is wide, and it should be. Trends over weeks are the thing to trust — not any single entry.

Do I need an API key?

Only for the automatic estimates and the diet assessment. Add a key from your provider of choice in Targets → settings and estimates start filling themselves in. Without one, the app drops into manual mode: you type calories and macros yourself and everything else — targets, day cards, history — works exactly the same.

Why two numbers instead of one?

Because the honest answer to “how many calories were in that?” is a range. Showing a single number invites you to chase a precision that was never there — and to panic over a 40-calorie overshoot that is well inside the noise. Days are judged against the range, with a ±50 calorie band counted as on target.

Is there an iPhone version?

Not as a native app yet. Lazy Macros is a web app wrapped for Android, so on iOS you can open it in Safari and add it to your home screen — everything except the native camera integration works the same.

What does it cost?

The app is free and open source. If you turn on automatic estimates you pay your AI provider directly for the tokens you use — typically fractions of a cent per logged food — and nothing to us.

Can I track something other than weight loss?

Yes. Set your goal to Maintain and the daily target becomes your TDEE. Protein targets are set independently, so you can run a maintenance or recomposition setup without a deficit.
